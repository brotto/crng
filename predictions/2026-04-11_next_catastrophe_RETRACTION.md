## TL;DR

On 2026-03-31, I publicly sealed a prediction that the next globally significant catastrophic event would occur between July 2026 and April 2027, with Q4 2026 as the peak probability window. The prediction was committed to this repository (commit `4453d93`), hashed (SHA-256 above), and published on X with reference to the hash as "proof of anteriority."

I am retracting that prediction today, 2026-04-11, 11 days after the seal and 811 days before the self-declared falsification window of 2028-06-30. The retraction is driven by the fact that **the method that produced the prediction is structurally broken in multiple ways** — one method contains a documented quantile-mapping bug, two other methods operate on the same historical data and are not independent evidence, and no part of the pipeline used information actually generated by the CRNG in a statistically valid way. The original falsification criterion was also biased toward confirmation: 48 of 63 non-overlapping 2-year windows in the 1900–2024 historical record (76.2%) contained at least one qualifying event. Waiting for the calendar to rule would have yielded "confirmation" with 3.2-to-1 odds (48:15) regardless of whether the method had predictive content.

The **original sealed file is preserved unchanged** in this repository. Its SHA-256 hash still validates. This retraction is an additive document — it does not rewrite history, does not delete the original, and does not break the cryptographic anteriority seal. What it removes is the epistemic standing of the claim. The paper trail stays for audit.

## Why retract now, and not wait for 2028-06-30

The original document included an explicit falsification criterion:

> *"A prediction is considered falsified if no event matching the above criteria occurs before 2028-06-30."*

That criterion looks epistemically virtuous. It is, in practice, biased toward confirmation. The "events matching the above criteria" are: any earthquake ≥ M6.7, any financial crash ≥ 15% drawdown in a major index, or any natural disaster with ≥ 1000 casualties or ≥ $10B damage — in a window of **2 years and 91 days** (from 2026-03-31 to 2028-06-30).

I ran the base-rate calculation against the same historical event list used to calibrate the prediction (39 earthquakes + 19 financial crashes + 24 natural disasters, 79 unique dates out of 82 raw events after deduplication). The calculation builds 63 non-overlapping 2-year windows with start dates from 1900-01-01 through 2024-01-01 (last complete window: 2024-01-01 through 2026-01-01) and counts how many contained at least one event meeting the criteria. The committed artifact is `benchmarks/retraction_2026-04-11/base_rate_2year_windows.json`.

Result:

| Quantity | Value |
|---|---|
| Non-overlapping 2-year windows, 1900-01-01 to 2024-12-31 | 63 |
| Windows containing ≥ 1 qualifying event | 48 |
| Windows empty | 15 |
| Base rate (fraction of windows with ≥ 1 event) | 48/63 = 0.7619 |

The base rate is **76.2%**, not 100%. The falsification criterion was therefore not statistically vacuous — roughly 1 in 4 historical 2-year windows would have triggered falsification. But the criterion was biased: a coin weighted 3.2-to-1 (48:15) toward confirmation cannot discriminate cleanly between a method that has predictive content and one that does not. If I had waited for 2028-06-30 and the window had closed with a qualifying event inside it, I would have had roughly a 76% chance of appearing to have predicted it purely by historical base rate, regardless of whether any method in the original document contributed signal. Symmetrically, if the window had closed empty, that would be a 24% event — consistent with pure bad luck, not evidence that the method was wrong.

The honest move is to retract when the method is known to be broken, not when the calendar rules on it. I became confident the method was broken on 2026-04-10 when the external Codex review (`REVIEWS/codex_review_2026-04.md`) identified the quantile-mapping bug in `experiments/next_catastrophe.py` and the confusion between descriptive and predictive use of the CRNG. I am filing this retraction one day later.

## The four structural defects

The sealed prediction was supported by four methods, presented in the original document as independent converging lines of evidence. The convergence is a structural illusion when the methods are audited individually.

### Defect 1 — CRNG-Modulated Sampling had a quantile-mapping bug

The second method, labeled "CRNG-Modulated Sampling," did the following (reconstructed from `experiments/next_catastrophe.py` as it stood at commit `4453d93`):

```python
rng = ContingencyRNG(seed=42, target_kurtosis=8.0, vol_clustering=0.35, n_oscillators=7)
for _ in range(n_simulations):
    crng_val = rng.next()
    quantile = min(max(crng_val, 0.001), 0.999)
    gap = np.percentile(gaps, quantile * 100)
    crng_next.append(gap)
```

This treats the output of `rng.next()` as a uniform in [0, 1] and passes it into `np.percentile`. But `ContingencyRNG.next()` does not return a quantile. It returns a log-return — a value centered at zero, with fat tails and variable std. The consequence, measured on 100,000 samples with the exact parameters above, is that the vast majority of samples fall below the 0.001 floor and are clipped to it. The frozen measurement is in `benchmarks/retraction_2026-04-11/clipping_measurement.json`:

| Bin | Count (n=100,000) | Fraction |
|---|---|---|
| Below 0.001 (clipped to floor) | 50,355 | 0.50355 |
| Above 0.999 (clipped to ceiling) | 496 | 0.00496 |
| In [0.001, 0.999] (valid range) | 49,149 | 0.49149 |

50.355% of samples were clipped to 0.001 and another 0.496% to 0.999. The "quantile" passed to `np.percentile(gaps, q*100)` is therefore dominated by the minimum historical gap (for the 50.355% of samples that hit the floor) and, to a lesser extent, the maximum (for the 0.496% that hit the ceiling). The resulting "CRNG-modulated" distribution is essentially a delta at `min_gap` with a second spike at `max_gap`. It has no relationship to either the CRNG output distribution or to the historical gap distribution.

The "CRNG-Modulated" prediction (median 2026-12-31, window Jul 2026 – Sep 2032) is therefore an artifact of clipping. This is documented in full in `REVIEWS/errata/2026-04-10_next_catastrophe_quantile_bug.md`, which now also cites the frozen measurement above.

**Method 2 is invalid. Its output should not be counted as evidence for anything.**

### Defect 2 — The "5.46×" spectral peak is real under iid noise but unusable as a predictive signal

The third method in the sealed document relied on a finding from the preceding `catastrophic_events.py` experiment: an FFT over the 78-gap sequence (derived from 79 unique event dates, from 82 raw events before deduplication) showing a maximum peak with power ratio 5.46× above the mean spectral power. The posts described this as "well above the 3× significance threshold" and as evidence of "quasi-periodic subliminal regularity."

I initially believed this would turn out to be routine spectral noise. Running 100,000 Monte Carlo trials of `max(|FFT[1:]|) / mean(|FFT[1:]|)` on iid standard-normal sequences of length 78 (artifact: `benchmarks/retraction_2026-04-11/fft_null_N78.json`) gives:

| Statistic | Value |
|---|---|
| Mean of max/mean ratio | 2.33 |
| 50th percentile | 2.29 |
| 95th percentile | 2.93 |
| 99th percentile | 3.30 |
| 99.9th percentile | 3.80 |
| Fraction of trials with ratio ≥ 3.0 | 0.0371 |
| Fraction of trials with ratio ≥ 5.46 | 0.00000 |

Under an iid Gaussian null, **zero of 100,000 trials** reached 5.46×. The observed value is well outside the iid null envelope. I was wrong when I first wrote that "peaks of 4–6× occur routinely." They do not, under iid noise for N=78.

This does not rescue the original inference. Three separate problems remain, and any one of them is enough to invalidate the predictive use of the spectral peak.

**Problem A — The "3× significance threshold" was invented post-hoc.** The iid null above shows the 99th percentile at 3.30, not 3.0. The 3.0 figure appeared in the posts with no citation, no null distribution, and no pre-registration. It is not a standard threshold in spectral analysis — it is a number the author chose after seeing the data. Under the actual iid null, 3.71% of trials would cross 3.0; the "significance threshold" would be a stratagem that produces false positives at 3.7%, not 5% or 1%.

**Problem B — The iid Gaussian null is the wrong null.** The 78-gap sequence is not iid Gaussian. It is a sequence of non-negative integer day counts derived from sorted, deduplicated historical event dates, with a hard minimum gap (no two events in a single day after deduplication) and with categorical structure (events come from three sources with different underlying rates). A sorted-gap sequence with a minimum-gap constraint has suppressed high-frequency variance and enhanced low-frequency structure compared to iid Gaussian, by construction. A null distribution with more low-frequency structure than iid Gaussian will tend to produce *larger* max-peak-to-mean FFT ratios than iid Gaussian does. The correct null is the distribution of max FFT peak ratios for a null model that preserves the same constraints — for example, a homogeneous Poisson process with the same total event count and time range, sampled and gap-transformed identically. That null has not been computed for this document, and computing it properly would require pre-registering the construction before looking at the data. **Comparing the observed 5.46× against an iid Gaussian null is therefore an upper bound on significance — it makes the peak look more extreme than it would under the correct null — not an appropriate test.** The observed value might still be extreme under the correct null, or it might not — the experiment does not discriminate.

**Problem C — Even if the spectral peak is real, it does not license calendar prediction.** The original document's Method 3 extrapolated the FFT's dominant period forward in time to estimate when the next event would occur. This is valid only if the underlying process is a genuine stationary periodic or quasi-periodic oscillator — a single rhythm that continues to beat with the same phase. There is no physical reason to expect a single periodic rhythm to govern the combined spacing of earthquakes, financial crashes, and natural disasters across 125 years. These are three categories of phenomena with unrelated causal mechanisms. A spectral peak in their combined gap sequence — even if it is beyond the noise envelope — describes where peaks were located in the historical record. It does not describe where the next event will be, any more than fitting a Fourier series to a noisy signal lets you extrapolate the signal beyond its support.

**Method 3 therefore fails in two ways:** the significance threshold was invented post-hoc against the wrong null, and the extrapolation from spectral peak to calendar forecast is mathematically unjustified regardless of whether the peak is "real."

(A chart showing the iid null distribution, the location of the 5.46× observation, and the per-problem failures — A, B, C — will be included in the forthcoming public errata in `posts/`.)

### Defect 3 — Bootstrap and Kaplan-Meier are not independent

The first method ("Empirical Bootstrap") and the fourth method ("Survival Analysis / Kaplan-Meier") both operate on the same 78 historical gaps, with no external information added. Bootstrap resamples the gaps and computes future gap quantiles; Kaplan-Meier constructs the empirical survival function over the same gaps and computes hazard rates. These are **two presentations of the same information**, not two independent tests. Their agreement is definitional: any two methods that build on the same empirical distribution of the same 78 points will yield consistent marginal summaries of that distribution.

Presenting them as two of four independent methods was a category error. Audited honestly, they count as one empirical-distribution-based prediction: "the next gap will resemble the previous 78 gaps." That is a defensible but extremely weak statement — weaker than any claim the original document made — and it requires no CRNG, no fat tails, no quasi-periodicity, no philosophy. A short `numpy` script produces the same result.

### Defect 4 — The CRNG contributed nothing

The CRNG's role in the four methods:

- **Method 1 (Bootstrap)**: does not use CRNG at all. Pure resampling of the historical gaps.
- **Method 2 (CRNG-Modulated)**: uses CRNG output as a quantile, which is the bug.
- **Method 3 (FFT)**: operates on the historical gap sequence, not on CRNG output. Does not use CRNG.
- **Method 4 (Kaplan-Meier)**: does not use CRNG. Standard survival analysis on the historical gaps.

**Only Method 2 actually touched the CRNG, and Method 2 was broken.** The three methods that produced coherent output were, in every respect, classical statistics over the historical series. Whatever predictive content was in the sealed document came from those classical methods — and, as established in Defect 3, those methods collapse to one restatement of the empirical distribution.

No part of the sealed prediction depended on CRNG's fat tails, volatility clustering, cascade amplification, or any other property that distinguishes CRNG from `numpy.random`. The narrative framing ("contingent encounters between potentialities," "Ποεσις," "para-ontological objects") was attached to the result post-hoc. It described nothing the numbers were actually doing.

Additionally, the "20/20 KS match" that the posts and the sealed document used as their headline result was measured on per-category gap sub-series. The per-category event counts are 39 (earthquakes), 19 (crashes), and 24 (disasters); the corresponding per-category gap counts are 38, 18, and 23. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test has well-known low power against the null of distributional similarity when sample sizes are this small. The "20/20 match" is therefore consistent with **the test being unable to detect differences that may exist**, not with the gap distributions being genuinely indistinguishable. A negative KS result on 18 gaps is not evidence of agreement; it is evidence of insufficient power.

## What the CRNG does, and does not, do

To separate the retraction from a broader claim I do not wish to make:

**What CRNG does (and is defended in the frozen benchmark of 2026-04):** It produces numerical sequences whose statistical fingerprint — excess kurtosis, frequency of tail events beyond 3σ, and volatility clustering — resembles real financial return series more closely than `numpy.random.default_rng().standard_normal()` does. On seven assets over five years, under a frozen snapshot and an a priori model-selection rule, CRNG wins 16 of 21 comparison cells. This result is **descriptive**, not predictive. It is documented in `benchmarks/snapshot_2026-04/frozen_benchmark_report.json`.

**What CRNG does not do:** It does not forecast the timing of future catastrophic events. It does not contain a causal model of earthquakes, financial crises, or natural disasters. It does not have access to any information about specific upcoming events. Its output cannot be transformed into a probability distribution over calendar dates without additional assumptions that the CRNG itself does not supply.

The sealed catastrophe prediction conflated these two categories. It took a descriptive generator whose validation is about distributional fingerprints and used it to produce a predictive statement about calendar time. This is exactly the confusion that principle P1 of `SPECS.md` — written as a response to this very incident — was formalized to prevent.

## What survives from the original document

Not nothing, but much less than what was claimed.

**Survives:** The observation that the coefficient of variation of the historical gap sequence (CV ≈ 0.94 for the combined 82-event, 79-unique-date series, as reported in the sealed document itself) is higher than the CV of gaps between iid Poisson events of the same mean rate. This is a **descriptive** observation about the statistical fingerprint of the historical series alone. It is consistent with the general CRNG thesis that real-world extreme events cluster more than Poisson predicts, but establishing that CRNG reproduces this fingerprint would require a separate frozen simulation experiment that has not been run within the scope of this retraction. The surviving claim is therefore restricted to the historical observation. It does not license any claim about *when* the next event will occur.

**Does not survive:** The 20/20 KS-match count (invalid because per-category KS tests were run on sample sizes of 18 to 38 gaps, at which the test has low power and failure-to-reject does not constitute evidence of agreement). The "5.46× FFT peak as significance" claim (invalid because the 3× threshold was invented post-hoc, the iid null was not the correct null to test against, and spectral peaks do not license calendar forecasts). The "convergence of four independent methods" (one method dressed four ways: bootstrap and Kaplan-Meier are the same information, FFT extrapolation is mathematically unjustified, CRNG-Modulated is broken). The calendar windows (July 2026 – April 2027, Q4 2026 peak, earthquake June 2026, and the per-category table). The hazard table (5.3% / 15.8% / 47.4% / 68.4% for 30/90/180/365 days). The self-declared falsification window of 2028-06-30. The philosophical synthesis that framed the numerical result as evidence for "subliminal linearity" or "para-ontological causation."

All of those are withdrawn.

## What this retraction does not claim

To be explicit about the limits of the retraction, so neither I nor future readers mistake one for the other:

1. **This retraction does not claim that CRNG is worthless.** The descriptive validation on financial returns stands. It is reported as 16/21 on seven assets in the frozen benchmark, and the language has been tightened to descriptive throughout `SPECS.md`, `REVIEWS/`, and the updated `README.md`.

2. **This retraction does not claim that the `catastrophic_events.py` experiment was entirely fraudulent.** It claims the *inference* from that experiment to a calendar prediction was invalid. The experiment itself — measuring gap distributions of real catastrophic events and comparing them to gap distributions of CRNG-generated extreme events — is a legitimate piece of exploratory data analysis, provided the interpretation is restricted to distributional shape and does not stretch to calendar time.

3. **This retraction does not claim that there is no structure in catastrophic event spacing.** It claims that the specific structure reported (5.46× spectral peak as significance, 20/20 KS match as evidence of indistinguishability) was not established by the evidence presented. Whether a larger sample, a properly null-compared spectral analysis, or a principled time-series model would find structure is an open question. The right answer for now is: unknown, and not pursued within this project.

4. **This retraction does not overwrite the git history.** Commit `4453d93` stays exactly where it is. The sealed prediction file stays exactly as it was. The SHA-256 hash still validates. Anyone who wants to audit what was claimed, when, and by whom, can do so with `shasum -a 256 predictions/2026-03-31_next_catastrophe.md` and compare against `33356f25c094746cc861ca7f77736ba6c8614ed83dfc2937389e7b068ffbfbbf`. This retraction is added alongside, not on top.

## How this retraction connects to the broader correction effort

On 2026-04-10, an external review (`REVIEWS/codex_review_2026-04.md`) identified three critical issues in the public presentation of this project: the "42/49 = 86%" benchmark was rolling, data-snooping, and not reproducible; the TSON expected-mesmitude formula was an invalid approximation; and the `next_catastrophe.py` quantile mapping was broken. The response to those findings was structural: `SPECS.md` (seven principles), the `compliance-officer` sub-agent (adversarial review of every public claim), and a new workflow of frozen benchmarks in `benchmarks/snapshot_2026-04/`.

The benchmark correction ran its own multi-round adversarial review cycle and was completed on 2026-04-10; its public record is `posts/benchmark_errata_2026-04-10.md` and its frozen evidence is `benchmarks/snapshot_2026-04/`. This retraction is the companion correction for the catastrophic-events claim. The TSON correction is the third and is in progress as of 2026-04-11.

The three retractions should be read as a single coherent correction of the 2026-Q1 public communications, not as three unrelated events. The common thread is the confusion between descriptive generation (what CRNG actually does) and predictive modeling (what I claimed CRNG was doing).
