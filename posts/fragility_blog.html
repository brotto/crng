The Problem With How We Measure Risk

The VIX sits at 24. Most risk managers would call that "elevated but manageable." The yield curve is normal (+0.71%). The S&P 500 is within one standard deviation of its 20-day mean. By every traditional metric, the system looks stressed but stable.

Traditional risk metrics assume returns follow a Gaussian distribution. They measure volatility (how wide the bell curve is) but ignore shape (how fat the tails are). This is the blind spot. A system can have moderate volatility and extreme fat tails simultaneously — meaning the average day looks normal, but the probability of catastrophic moves is orders of magnitude higher than models predict.

The CRNG-Fragility Monitor doesn't look at volatility. It looks at kurtosis — the statistical measure of tail fatness. And what it's showing right now is unprecedented.

The CRNG doesn't model the mechanics of collapse. It detects the statistical signature of systems approaching one — like hearing the frequency of a bridge vibration shift before it snaps.

What This Is Not

This is not a prediction that markets will crash tomorrow. Kurtosis measures the shape of the distribution, not its direction. High kurtosis means extreme moves in either direction are more likely than models assume. It's a measure of fragility, not destiny.

What it does tell us is that the current regime is statistically unlike anything in the last 18 years. The system is not in equilibrium. Fat tails mean that the models most institutions use to manage risk are systematically underestimating the probability of extreme events.
